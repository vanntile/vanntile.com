I think [last time][eu-is-for-encryption] I wasn't clear enough, so I will say it again:
[Chat Control][tuta-chat-control], or any similarly misinformed legislation proposals, must not pass in the European
Union or in the world at large. Once you get the bureaucratic apparatus moving, it becomes quite impossible to
immediately stop a chaotic, badly designed, prone-to-abuse and ineffective in its goals surveillance system.

## Why now?

This time it's Denmark pushing it, actively holding the EU Council Presidency, and, as a country with experience in
surveillance (member of the Nine Eyes as per various statements post the Snowden leaks of 2013), it is unsurprising how
their Minister of Justice could very well fit in the orwellian Ministry of Love with statements like this:

> we have to break with the totally erroneous view that it is every man's freedom to communicate on encrypted
> messaging services
> - Peter Hummelgaard, as per [Question 1426][danish-consultation-question-1426]

And if you thought that wasn't clear enough, the answer to the question in the thread is quite revealing:

> The Minister's statement reflects a concern that encrypted messaging services are being exploited for criminal
> activities, such as the recruitment of young people into gangs and serious crimes. While the response does not
> explicitly name iMessage or WhatsApp, the Minister’s position implies skepticism toward the idea that end-to-end
> encryption in any form—regardless of platform—should be considered an absolute civil liberty, especially when it
> hinders law enforcement.

So yeah, in his opinion, the right to privacy exists only in relation to each other (for now) and not to the state, one
of the two major forces most readily interested in capitalizing and controlling on all your information. Don't worry,
Chat Control, if implemented as intended, will enlarge the group represented by the state to include a lot of
inexistent infrastructure until you would be better communicating only publicly. Maybe the panopticon was the friends
we made along the way.

## You have the right to not say anything about it, or ever again

Just for reference, the **EU Charter of Fundamental Rights** specifies in Article 7:

> Everyone has the right to respect for his or her private and family life, home and communications.

Wondering if this should be actually compulsory reading material to a Ministry of Justice. While this article includes
provisions regarding the "interests of national security, public safety" and others, I would add that Article 8
(Protection of personal data) specifies that:

> Such data must be processed fairly for specified purposes and on the basis of the consent of the person concerned or
some other legitimate basis laid down by law

And is this lack of fairness and vaugeness of the Chat Control documents that I try to help you all understand that are
complete overreach of institutional powers. Don't let yourself fooled, as a technical person, I am well aware that for
any centralized system such as this, you will need an infrastructure (in people, servers and procedures) that far
exceeds the abilities of the Union, but that doesn't matter at all when they do not understand that nobody possesses
[such an automation][eu-commission-chat-control-does-not-exist] (and likely never will) and what even small error rates
will do to the situations. The following is a fragment of my own message towards MEPs the last time this was on the
table illustrating the numbers for an accuracy of 99.99% (the real rate being closer to 51.7% as seen from the optional
German Chat Control 1.0 [implementation][german-implementation] for 2024 resulting in 99375 conversations or images of
innocents sent to the police).

With a population of 450 million, 90% using the Internet regularly (and 44% lacking basic digital skills) and 82% using
instant messaging, if one is to assume 4 pieces of media sent daily (an significant understatement to the most prolific
younger cohort of the population which could, on aggregate, send thousands of text messages and hundreds of pieces of
media), and an error rate of 1 per 10000 (presented to you as 99.99% accurate/false positive rate), it would result in
73 thousand false leads every day that police departments would need to investigate, taking time from the respective
departments to go for real crime. To go into detail even more, simulating the scenario above, in a little a bit over a
year (371 days), one percent of the whole population of EU would need to be investigated at least twice (repeated false
accusations), which would not bode well with anyone. This (again, underwhelmingly low) estimate would result in so many
false positives daily which would need to be contacted directly and urgently (and without alarming the suspects) by the
police if the system is built in its most secure version and run completely on users' devices. If it doesn't and sends
data to the investigative bodies (or whoever runs this infrastructure in your country), the police effort will be
drastically reduced and the hacking and blackmail (when others will threaten to send or add illicit material to one's
device even if that would pose no risk to the recipient) risks will be incalculably increased.

Who is going to be the independent authority that shall verify the compliance of what each officer and clerk of each
police department of every country in EU will be doing with this access. It wouldn't be the first time an EU
institution forces another to pay up because they are not following data processing laws.

Finally, remember this automatically turns you into a second class citizen, as organs of the state are exempt from this
surveillance. Equality at its finest.

## Act now

I ask you to contact your MEPs, especially if you are represented in Germany, as they currently stand as the deciding
power in the upcoming vote on October 14th. The effort is not that big, as you can use the
[Fight Chat Control][fight-chat-control] website for the most general list of concerns and the email addresses and
currently known positions of all EU representatives. In an ideal case, we would not need to get to the stage of
parliementary vote and this worst version so far of the bill would be canceled by the EU Council vote, but we'd better
prepare for the worst without panicking. This is an attack on rights during unfavourable times, and, beyond specific
issues with this one project, the reason why nothing dangerous should be allowed without restraint to today's leaders
"because they are the good guys" is that it becomes an invaluable asset for when "the bad guys" become in charge.

## Let's get chatting

I will be using  this section to express my very specific thoughts on actual excerpts from the
[Interinstitutional File 2022/0155 (COD) from 1 July 2025 (pdf)][proposal-july-2025], which outlines the current state
of the Chat Control, as per my own knowledge. What I want to underline is that under a well-intentioned veil we can
find contradicting statements, lack of technological understanding, making of baseless expectations and, last of all,
hypocrisy. Let's begin.

> Detection in interpersonal communications services using end-to-end encryption is enabled prior to the transmission
> of content requiring the users’ consent; Technologies used for detection have to be vetted with regard to their
> effectiveness, their impact on fundamental rights and risks to cyber security, and be approved by implementing act
> with specific safeguards applying to technologies for detection in services using end-to-end encryption;

I totally agree, let's vet the detection methods according to those criteria: accuracy on small datasets (or small
pilots) between 50 and 90%, and in existing implementations "only 25% of a quality the police can work on"
([source - EU Commissioner Johansson][commissioner-johansson]), an unreasonable loss of fundamental rights for a
non-effective outcome and a full undermining of cyber security. I guess by safeguards you would refer to the, again,
**non-existent** infrastructure where all this would be reported, recorded or accessible on. If this is meant to be
run by any … american tech giant… this would be in direct contradiction with the current aim of EU digital sovereignty.

> The measures taken by providers of hosting services and providers of publicly available interpersonal communications
> services to execute detection orders addressed to them should remain strictly limited to what is specified in this
> Regulation and in the detection orders issued in accordance with this Regulation.

And even if you do ensure they restrict themselves to this regulation, just imagine the glorious scale of the first
time they have a security problem. What was before an end-to-end encrypted app becomes a large-scale, individually
identifiable (did I mention this needs to fix identity at scale too), distributed database (so hard to track
exfiltration from) of all your info.

> In order to ensure the effectiveness of those measures, allow for tailored solutions, remain technologically neutral,
> and avoid circumvention of the detection obligations, those measures should be taken regardless of the technologies
> used by the providers concerned in connection to the provision of their services. Therefore, this Regulation leaves
> to the provider concerned the choice of the technologies to be operated to comply effectively with detection orders
> and should not be understood as incentivising or disincentivising the use of any given technology, provided that the
> technologies and accompanying measures meet the requirements of this Regulation.

Aaaaaah, I finally get it, you won't have one eye in the digital sky, you will have one of those biblically accurate
angels - one eye with many eyes under it. Each corporate entity holding one of these services - Meta (three times, as
it has multiple incompatible apps), Apple, Telegram, Signal, Discord, and at least a dozen smaller ones, each
implementing whatever they see fit, under whatever guarantee they can prove follows arbitrary standards. If they can
argument it as such, it shall pass.

> That includes the use of end-to-end encryption technology, which is an important tool to guarantee the security and
> confidentiality of the communications of users, including those of children.

So true, but so misinterpreted. Once again, say it with me - if private communication is outlawed, only criminals will
have private communication. This, if put in action, has dozens of ways of backfiring against the very children it wants
to save: more shady, less secure apps becoming the target for groomers, malicious sharing of pictures between underage
teens to intentionally trigger these algorithms and an increase distrust in authorities and desensitization against
abuses of their own privacy. If it is normal for someone not invited in their texts to read them, then what harm can
one more do? Again, all this automated scanning solution is fundamentally different from a reporting system, which
several apps already have.

> Having regard to the availability of technologies that can be used to meet the requirements of this Regulation whilst
> still allowing for end-to-end encryption, nothing in this Regulation should be interpreted as prohibiting, weakening
> or circumventing, requiring to disable, or making end-to-end encryption impossible.

I reserve my right to interpret what you say as it is. This is not a denial of circumventing security, but a denial of
your right to call it so.

> Providers should remain free to offer services using end-to-end encryption and should not be obliged by this
> Regulation to decrypt data or create access to end-to-end encrypted data.

But this is EXACTLY what you are asking, just because you think a loophole of "data is not yet encrypted so it's
free to scan".

> When executing the detection order, providers should take all available safeguard measures to ensure that the
> technologies employed by them cannot be used by them or their employees for purposes other than compliance with this
> Regulation, nor by third parties, and thus to avoid undermining cybersecurity and the confidentiality of the
> communications of users, while ensuring the effective detection of online child sexual abuse and the fair balance
> of all the fundamental rights at stake. To avoid the significant impairment of cybersecurity, providers should
> identify, analyse and assess the possible cybersecurity risks derived from the implementation of the technologies
> used to execute the detection order and put in place the necessary mitigation measures to minimise such risks.

If the providers take this seriously, and bring in cybersecurity experts and privacy advocates, there is a single
solution. Manual, user-provided reports (sending the last n messages to the provider for scanning) and no automated
scanning of all your messages. But that would be a totally different regulation, one I actually would see as positive.

> While end-to-end encryption is a necessary means of protecting fundamental rights and the digital security of
> governments, industry and society, the European Union needs to ensure the effective prevention of and fight against
> serious crime such as child sexual abuse.

 Why would the EU take effective action such as actually removing explicit material from where it is hosted or even try
 to catch perpertrators? They definitely do not share their loot with others, especially on… Whatsapp… Be honest for a
 second, you risk criminalizing the teens, removing any sentiment of digital safety from kids (and later adults) and
 any expectation of privacy in life. You are not bringing effective prevention, you are making groomers' work easier
 by normalizing the lack of privacy.

> Therefore, child sexual abuse material should remain detectable in all interpersonal communications services through
> the application of vetted technologies, when uploaded, under the condition that the users give their explicit consent
> under the provider’s terms and conditions for a specific technology being applied to such detection in the respective
> service. Users not giving their consent should still be able to use that part of the service that does not involve
> the sending of visual content and URLs.

Therefore, user consent is still needed (that's good), but feature sets must be restricted to prevent the case that
this regulation argues for. This, once again, assumes these legitimate services are the main ones where CSEM is
distributed, and not that the risk to children comes from grooming. I'm afraid that this would become prime target for
modification for future regulations, when the surveillance infrastructure will be in place, but they won't be able to
argue for any significant improvement in child safety. Remember, any current evaluation of the effectivenes of such
methods is despite major lack of data, in spite of professional warnings and focusing on what can become valid
investigation for police officers, thus the actual actionable rate is an even smaller share of that.

Either way, this part of the document, if followed through, offers the only valid option to a security and privacy
minded persion. Refusal of installing any of such automation on one's own device and day-to-day usage in text form
only for encrypted communication. Technology is evolving, just backwards.

> This ensures that the detection mechanism can access the data in its unencrypted form for effective analysis and
> action, without compromising the protection provided by end-to-end encryption once the data is transmitted. To avoid
> the weakening of the protection provided by the encryption, technologies intended to be used for detection in
> services using end-to-end encryption should be certified by the EU Centre and tested with the support of its
> Technology Committee before undergoing the vetting procedure foreseen for all detection technologies.

The amount of corporate focus, power that it's given to them and responsibility dispersion makes me incredibly uneasy.
Let's remind ourselves that data aggregators and big tech would rather abuse the system and pay a fine than refuse to
engage in legally dubious actions. [Again][apple-rather-pay-fine] and [again][microsoft-will-pay-fine].

> In interpersonal communications services using end-to-end encryption, those technologies shall detect the
> dissemination of child sexual abuse material prior to its transmission

If we let all other arguments slide for a moment, I doubt currently one would be to run such a service on device and
privately. It would need either significant amount of storage for a majority if not the full database of known hashes,
or a pseudo-private system which asks for more hashes than needed to cover one specific identification gap, which
still leak information, but reduce certainty. Finally, there is a sever underestimation of the processing power of
mobile devices, with the most likely scenarios being effectively bricking them or never finishing a report on any
video (which is likely a more prevalent CSEM format).

> The technologies shall be:
>   (a) be effective and suitable in detecting the dissemination of known or new child sexual abuse material or the
> solicitation of children, as applicable;
>   (aa) not introduce cybersecurity risks for which it is not possible to take any effective measures to mitigate
> such risk;
>   (ab) if applied in services using end-to-end encryption, be certified by the EU Centre following tests conducted
> with the support of its Technology Committee, that their use could not lead to a weakening of the protection
> provided by the encryption;

Very hard for me to square the round peg of how one would identify solicitation if only looking at visual content and
links and not text. This section must be removed no matter the result of this regulation. The second point makes it
clear that this regulation should not be acted upon and comes into contradiction with expert knowledge regarding the
rest of this document. The third is the biggest possibility of abuse and lobbying. The people evaluating this decision
will likely be the last bastion of hope when it comes to EU citizen's privacy.

> By … \[three years from the date of the entry into force of this Regulation], and if necessary, every three years
> thereafter, the Commission shall submit a report to the European Parliament and the Council assessing the necessity
and feasibility of including the detection of the solicitation of children in the scope of Section 2 of Chapter II of
this Regulation. The assessment shall include an analysis of the state of development and readiness of the technologies
to detect the solicitation of children, including within interpersonal communications services using end-to-end
encryption, and error rates

It's good that tech moves slowly and three years is not actually like a decade and a half in digital spaces. I find
this non-continuous evaluation system rizible and proof that we are not going to be able to stop this surveillance
machine once on its feet. Stop it now. Contact your MEP or even all MEPs willing to engage with public conversation.
